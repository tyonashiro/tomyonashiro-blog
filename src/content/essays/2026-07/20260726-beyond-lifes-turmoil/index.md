# Beyond Life’s Turmoil

2026-07-26

![](cristian-palmer-XexawgzYOBc-unsplash.jpg)

## The Place Where We Usually Live

A difficult conversation can occupy an entire day. A careless remark may remain in the mind long after the person who made it has forgotten. A disappointment at work changes the atmosphere of the evening. A disagreement at home follows us into the next morning. Even success brings only temporary relief before another concern takes its place.

Most people live within this continuous movement of reaction. We respond to praise and criticism, closeness and rejection, achievement and failure, hope and uncertainty. Our attention moves from one event to another, and each event demands to be treated as the most important reality in our lives.

There is nothing abnormal about this. Human beings are social, emotional, and vulnerable. We depend upon other people, material conditions, physical health, and institutions that remain partly outside our control. We cannot avoid being affected by what happens around us.

The deeper difficulty begins when the strongest event of the moment becomes the center of the self. A disappointment no longer feels like something that happened. It becomes evidence that life itself has failed. A conflict no longer remains a disagreement. It becomes a judgment on our worth. Fear about the future becomes the atmosphere through which every part of the present is interpreted.

We begin to live on the surface of experience, where every wave appears large enough to define the whole ocean.

James Allen used the phrase *Above Life’s Turmoil* to describe the possibility of living from another center. The title can sound as though a person should rise beyond ordinary problems and become untouched by them. Yet Allen’s wider image is closer to the depth of the ocean beneath a storm. The waves remain active at the surface, but they do not represent the full reality of the sea.

This image captures an experience that appears across religious, philosophical, and psychological traditions. Human beings have repeatedly discovered that suffering becomes more destructive when the self is located entirely within it. The circumstances may be real, painful, and resistant to change, but they do not need to become the final definition of reality.

A person can suffer without becoming identical to suffering. One can be disappointed without allowing disappointment to determine the meaning of an entire life. One can experience anger without making anger the permanent home of consciousness. The event remains, but the center from which it is experienced begins to move.

Such movement does not provide a magic formula for removing difficulty. A broken relationship may still require repair or separation. Financial problems still require practical action. Illness still demands treatment. Injustice still calls for courage and resistance. Wisdom does not free us from responsibility by declaring the visible world unimportant.

It offers another form of freedom. It allows us to participate in the conditions of life without surrendering the whole self to them.

## Different Languages for the Same Human Discovery

Many traditions describe this movement, although they do not explain it in the same way. Their similarities can be striking, especially when they speak about suffering, attachment, meaning, faith, self-control, or inner peace. Yet their differences matter because each tradition begins with a different understanding of the human person and the nature of reality.

Buddhism begins with the recognition of *dukkha*, the instability and dissatisfaction woven into conditioned existence. It does not dismiss suffering as imaginary. Pain, illness, aging, separation, grief, and death remain real features of life. Buddhism directs attention toward the additional suffering created by craving, clinging, and the demand that changing conditions provide permanent security.

The teaching of non-self does not ask a person to believe that nothing exists or that individual life has no value. It challenges the assumption that there is a permanent, independent, and fully controllable self at the center of experience. The body changes. Feelings arise and pass away. Thoughts appear without invitation. Identities formed through work, relationships, nationality, success, and memory remain dependent upon conditions.

Freedom begins when these changing elements are no longer grasped as an unchanging possession. Pain may still arise, but the mind becomes less compelled to build an entire identity around it. There is sadness, but sadness does not have to become “who I am.” There is anger, but anger need not become the permanent voice through which the world is understood.

Christianity approaches suffering from another direction. Its center is not detachment from all identity but relationship with God. Human dignity rests neither on success nor on the approval of others, but on being created, known, and loved by God. Present circumstances can wound the person deeply, yet they cannot provide the final account of that person’s existence.

The Christian claim that the Kingdom of God is within or among us does not reduce faith to a private psychological state. It points toward a reality that is already present while also exceeding the individual. Christian peace emerges through trust, grace, hope, worship, and participation in a community that remembers a story larger than the present moment.

This peace is not emotional invulnerability. The Bible contains lament, fear, anger, abandonment, and grief. Christ himself weeps, experiences anguish, and cries out from the cross. Faith does not remove suffering from consciousness. It refuses to grant suffering ultimate authority.

James Allen, Stoicism, and related traditions place greater emphasis on the governance of thought and character. They distinguish external circumstances from the judgments, habits, and reactions through which those circumstances are interpreted. Their language often sounds more self-directed. The individual is called to cultivate discipline, examine thought, and develop a character that does not rise and fall with every change in fortune.

This approach can become too severe when it implies that all suffering results from incorrect thinking. Human beings are affected by trauma, poverty, violence, illness, discrimination, and social conditions that cannot be overcome through attitude alone. Still, the insight remains valuable. The mind contributes to the form that suffering takes, and inner discipline can prevent a difficult circumstance from spreading into every region of life.

Viktor Frankl offers another perspective. His work does not claim that meaning removes pain or guarantees survival. His experience in the Nazi concentration camps cannot be reduced to a lesson about positive thinking. Countless people with courage, faith, and purpose were murdered. Survival depended on many conditions beyond the control of any prisoner.

Frankl’s contribution lies in his claim that even under extreme constraint, a person may retain some freedom in responding to what cannot be changed. Meaning can be found through love, responsibility, work, memory, sacrifice, and the attitude taken toward unavoidable suffering. Such meaning does not make evil acceptable. It prevents evil from occupying the whole interior world.

These traditions are not interchangeable. Buddhism does not teach Christian salvation in different language. Christianity does not reduce faith to psychological resilience. Frankl does not offer a complete theology or metaphysics. James Allen’s moral psychology cannot replace either social analysis or clinical care.

They illuminate different dimensions of the same human condition. Buddhism examines clinging. Christianity speaks of relationship, grace, and hope. Stoicism and James Allen emphasize judgment and character. Frankl focuses on meaning and responsibility. Their shared discovery is that the center of human life need not remain fixed within immediate circumstance.

## The Many Homes of the Ego

The ego is often described as though it were a single obvious thing, a selfish voice that can be identified and overcome. In practice, it is far more adaptable. It can attach itself to almost anything, including the effort to become free from ego.

A person may identify with possessions, social position, professional success, physical strength, nationality, religion, political belief, personal suffering, or the approval of a community. These attachments are easy to recognize when they appear in exaggerated form. More difficult attachments can grow from knowledge, virtue, spiritual practice, and intellectual maturity.

Decades of studying philosophy do not automatically make a person wise. Familiarity with theology does not guarantee compassion. Meditation can become a source of pride. Psychological vocabulary can be used to judge others from a position of superiority. Even humility can become an image one wishes others to admire.

The ego does not always resist spiritual development. It can appropriate it.

Spiral Dynamics provides a useful map for understanding how human values respond to changing life conditions. Its stages describe recurring patterns of survival, belonging, power, order, achievement, equality, and systemic integration. Each value system emerges because it answers real needs that earlier systems could not manage adequately.

At the Beige level, survival becomes primary. Food, shelter, physical safety, and immediate bodily needs dominate attention. Purple creates belonging through family, tribe, ritual, ancestry, and shared protection. Red asserts power, courage, independence, and personal will. Blue establishes order through duty, discipline, moral law, tradition, and transcendent authority. Orange advances through reason, science, competition, progress, and individual achievement. Green emphasizes equality, inclusion, care, dialogue, and sensitivity to marginalized voices.

None of these stages is reducible to a mistake. Each contains capacities required by human life. We need survival instincts during danger, loyalty within families, assertiveness when boundaries are violated, discipline for long-term commitments, reason for solving complex problems, and compassion for sustaining community.

Each stage also contains characteristic traps. Survival consciousness can narrow the world to immediate necessity. Tribal loyalty can become fear of outsiders. Strength can become domination. Moral order can harden into dogmatism. Achievement can treat human beings as instruments. Compassion can become unable to distinguish understanding from agreement or kindness from the refusal to set limits.

Suffering appears within every stage because every value system encounters conditions it cannot fully control. A person may endure hardship for the sake of family, drawing strength from Purple belonging. Another may rely on Red determination, Blue faith, Orange problem-solving, or Green solidarity. These strategies can all sustain human beings through difficulty, but none creates permanent immunity from fear, loss, conflict, or self-deception.

Second-tier thinking is sometimes imagined as a final spiritual state above these earlier forms of consciousness. Such an interpretation turns development into a status hierarchy. People begin describing themselves as Yellow or Turquoise while classifying those around them as lower-level minds.

The model then becomes another home for the ego.

A more responsible understanding of development follows the principle of inclusion and transcendence. Later capacities do not eliminate earlier ones. They place them within a wider field of judgment. A mature person still needs the survival intelligence of Beige, the belonging of Purple, the courage of Red, the discipline of Blue, the rationality of Orange, and the compassion of Green. The task is to recognize which capacity is appropriate to the conditions rather than allowing one system to dominate every situation.

Religious traditions cannot be assigned permanently to Blue or Green. A religion may express Blue order through doctrine, duty, and institutional authority. The same tradition may express Purple community, Red power, Orange organizational ambition, Green compassion, or second-tier integration. Secular institutions display the same range.

A Christian may obey rules from fear, pursue influence through religious identity, serve the poor through compassion, study scripture through rational inquiry, or integrate several perspectives while remaining grounded in faith. A Buddhist institution may protect tradition, exercise political power, support social justice, or cultivate contemplative insight. The label placed upon a tradition does not determine the level of consciousness through which it is lived.

Development therefore cannot be separated from humility. Greater complexity does not make a person morally superior. It increases the number of perspectives one may be able to hold, but it also creates more sophisticated ways of avoiding self-examination.

## Remembering Is Not Enough

Human beings do not suffer from a shortage of wisdom. Many people have heard more good advice than they could apply in several lifetimes. Religious communities repeat teachings about forgiveness, humility, patience, charity, and trust. Philosophers teach self-examination. Psychologists explain projection, trauma, attachment, and emotional regulation. Books, lectures, retreats, and online discussions place centuries of insight within easy reach.

Yet a person can listen to a moving homily about love and begin quarreling on the way home from church. Someone can finish a book about compassion and respond harshly to the next interruption. A meditation session may produce a sense of clarity that disappears during the first stressful meeting of the day.

The Epistle of James compares the hearer who fails to act with someone who looks into a mirror, walks away, and immediately forgets what was seen. The image captures a central difficulty of moral and spiritual life. We do not forget the words in a literal sense. We forget the condition of awareness in which those words appeared true.

During worship, study, meditation, or reflection, the mind becomes receptive to a wider perspective. The self is temporarily released from its usual pressures. The teaching feels obvious because attention has been gathered around it.

Daily life then returns with its speed, fatigue, conflict, and competing demands. Old habits regain control before reflection has time to intervene. The person who understood forgiveness in church becomes the person who feels insulted in the car. The knowledge remains available, but it no longer governs perception.

This gap between understanding and conduct explains the repetitive structure of wisdom traditions. Prayer is repeated. Scripture is read again. The same seasons return in the liturgical calendar. Buddhists return to the breath and the precepts. Stoics examine the day and prepare for the next. Monastic communities organize life around recurring hours of work, silence, worship, and study.

Repetition is not evidence that these traditions lack new ideas. It reflects an accurate understanding of human memory. We forget existentially long before we forget intellectually.

A truth can remain stored in the mind while disappearing from the way we see. We know that anger distorts judgment, yet we trust every judgment produced by anger. We know that possessions cannot guarantee security, yet we organize life around acquiring more. We know that other people carry burdens invisible to us, yet we interpret their behavior as a personal offense.

The first task of wisdom is therefore remembrance. We need practices that return attention to what daily movement causes us to lose. Yet remembering alone does not complete the work.

A person can remember an entire tradition and remain unchanged. Knowledge can become verbal fluency without transformation. We may quote scripture, explain Buddhist concepts, describe developmental stages, and analyze our own psychological patterns while continuing to act from resentment, fear, pride, and wounded self-importance.

The mirror shows us something true, but we can also become attached to the image of ourselves as people who understand mirrors.

## The Discipline of Unlearning

Learning expands the world. It gives names to experiences that once seemed confusing. It introduces distinctions, histories, arguments, and forms of understanding that allow us to see beyond inherited assumptions. Long study can deepen judgment and protect us from simplistic claims.

Yet learning carries a hidden risk. Knowledge can become another structure of identity. The person no longer says only, “I have studied this subject.” The deeper message becomes, “I am the kind of person who knows.”

Once knowledge supports identity, correction begins to feel like injury. Questions sound like challenges to status. New evidence becomes threatening. Other people are evaluated according to how closely they approach the conclusions we have already reached.

Unlearning is the discipline that keeps knowledge alive.

It does not require abandoning education, forgetting facts, or treating every opinion as equally valid. Nor does it mean returning to a state of innocence before experience. Unlearning loosens the attachment between what we know and who we believe ourselves to be.

A person may hold a conviction firmly while remaining open to correction. One may speak with confidence without needing to dominate the conversation. Experience can guide action without becoming a claim to infallibility. Mature knowledge becomes more precise because it no longer has to protect the ego at every point.

Christianity expresses this openness through poverty of spirit. The phrase does not praise ignorance or lack of ability. It recognizes that before God, no amount of study turns the human person into a self-sufficient authority. Faith begins with receptivity. Wisdom is received as grace before it becomes an achievement.

Zen speaks of beginner’s mind, the capacity to approach experience without allowing established categories to determine everything in advance. Socrates grounds philosophy in the recognition that awareness of ignorance is more truthful than false certainty. Scientific inquiry advances through a related willingness to let evidence correct theory.

These traditions arise from different foundations, yet they share a discipline of openness. Learning without unlearning tends to harden. It becomes a collection of answers defended against reality rather than a means of encountering reality more fully.

Unlearning also changes the relationship between humility and confidence. Humility is often confused with hesitation, low self-esteem, or reluctance to speak. Confidence is often confused with certainty and self-assertion. Mature humility and mature confidence support each other.

A humble person can act decisively because action does not depend upon proving personal superiority. A confident person can admit error because correction does not destroy the foundation of identity. One can say, “This is what I understand and why I believe it,” while also saying, “I may need to revise my judgment.”

Such freedom is difficult because daily life rewards fixed identities. Professional roles encourage expertise. Social media encourages positions. Political and religious communities reward loyalty to established language. People become known through what they defend, oppose, and represent.

Unlearning interrupts this pressure. It allows the self to become less invested in maintaining an image. The need to appear wise begins to weaken. The need to win every disagreement loses some of its urgency. One can listen without preparing a reply and change without experiencing change as humiliation.

Years of learning remain valuable. They become more valuable when carried with open hands.

The result is not passivity. Unlearning does not ask us to stand apart from suffering, injustice, or responsibility. It makes engagement more truthful because action arises less from the need to defend an identity. Humility clears the vision. Confidence gives the courage to act upon what is seen.

## Living Below the Surface

Life does not become easier because we have studied wisdom. Relationships remain vulnerable to misunderstanding. Work continues to involve pressure, rivalry, and uncertainty. Bodies age. People we love become ill. Plans fail. Communities divide. Political and economic forces shape lives in ways that no individual can fully control.

A deeper center does not prevent these experiences. It changes the proportion they occupy within consciousness.

The ocean image helps because depth and surface belong to the same body of water. To live below the surface does not mean leaving the waves behind. The person still feels fear, anger, grief, desire, affection, and hope. Wisdom does not divide human beings into an untouched spiritual depth and an unimportant emotional surface.

The movement goes in both directions. Depth steadies our response to the surface, while the surface reveals whether depth has become real. The measure of spiritual insight is not the feeling produced during a sermon, retreat, book, or meditation. It appears in how a person responds during conflict, disappointment, fatigue, and uncertainty.

A calm center should not be confused with emotional flatness. There are situations in which anger is the appropriate response. Grief honors what has been lost. Fear warns of danger. Shame may reveal a need for repentance. Confidence may require strong resistance against manipulation or injustice.

The question concerns whether these states serve life or possess it. Anger can identify a violated boundary without becoming hatred. Grief can remain faithful to love without closing the future. Fear can encourage preparation without governing every decision. Strength can protect without becoming domination.

This is where the different traditions meet without losing their identities. Buddhism loosens the clinging that converts passing experience into a permanent self. Christianity locates the person within the love and promise of God. Frankl turns attention toward meaning, responsibility, and the future. James Allen and Stoicism cultivate the judgment and character required to remain steady. Spiral Dynamics reminds us that every situation may call upon different human capacities.

No single perspective provides the whole answer. Together, they show that transcendence is not escape. It is a change in the relationship between the self and the conditions of life.

We still manage the problem. We apologize, forgive, resist, negotiate, seek treatment, establish boundaries, leave harmful situations, rebuild trust, and accept what cannot be restored. Inner peace does not replace action. It protects action from being controlled entirely by panic, resentment, pride, or despair.

The mature life contains a continuing rhythm of learning, remembering, and unlearning. We learn because ignorance limits perception. We remember because daily movement pulls us back into habit. We unlearn because even wisdom can become a possession that separates us from reality.

These movements do not form a ladder from which we leave earlier stages behind. They return throughout life. A new situation exposes an old attachment. A familiar teaching acquires a meaning we could not see before. A failure removes an illusion of self-sufficiency. A relationship reveals how much of our generosity depended upon recognition.

Each return can deepen freedom. We become less surprised by our capacity to forget and less eager to construct a superior identity around what we know. We learn to begin again without treating beginning again as defeat.

The person who lives beyond life’s turmoil is not someone who has escaped the world. It is someone who no longer asks every circumstance to supply identity, security, and meaning. Such a person can remain fully present to the demands of life because the whole weight of the self is not resting upon the outcome of each event.

The waves continue to move. Some are beautiful, and some are destructive. They deserve attention because they are part of the sea in which we live. Yet beneath them remains a depth that cannot be represented by any single storm.

Wisdom teaches us to remember that depth. Unlearning teaches us not to claim it as a possession. Humility keeps us open to reality, and confidence allows us to meet it without retreat.

We do not become free by ceasing to care. We become free enough to care without being consumed.
