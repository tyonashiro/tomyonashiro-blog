# The View We Cannot Possess

2026-08-16

![](john-schaidler-9V3Q2W_mRLE-unsplash.jpg)

## The World Seen from Within

A religious worldview is rarely experienced by a devout believer as one interpretation among many. From within the faith, it appears as reality itself. It explains the origin of the universe, the meaning of suffering, the demands of morality, the destiny of the human person, and the proper organization of communal life. Belief does not occupy one compartment of existence. It gives form to existence as a whole.

For much of human history, people did not choose their religion after comparing several traditions as modern consumers might compare philosophies or ways of life. They were born into a sacred order sustained by family, community, law, ritual, and political authority. The gods of the community, or the God worshipped by it, belonged to the structure of the world into which a person awakened. To question that order could feel less like adopting a different opinion and more like leaving reality behind.

This condition was not limited to religion. A language, a social hierarchy, a national mythology, or an inherited understanding of gender and family could also appear natural because no serious alternative was visible. The surrounding world confirmed itself. Everyone whose judgment mattered seemed to share the same assumptions, and institutions carried those assumptions from one generation to the next.

People outside this moral universe were therefore difficult to recognize as equals. They could be described as pagans, heretics, barbarians, infidels, or members of an inferior civilization. Such words did more than identify differences. They placed outsiders beyond the boundaries of full moral concern. Violence against them could be justified as the defense of truth, order, or divine command.

Religious wars have never been caused by doctrine alone. Territory, political authority, trade, dynastic ambition, and collective resentment have often been involved. Yet religious certainty can give these conflicts a sacred intensity. When opponents represent not only a competing interest but an offense against the order of existence, compromise begins to look like betrayal. Cruelty acquires moral permission.

Modern societies often imagine that they have outgrown this condition. Many people now encounter several religions, cultures, and moral systems during an ordinary day. Information from across the world reaches us through the devices in our hands. We know, at least intellectually, that billions of people live within histories different from our own.

Yet the old enclosure has not disappeared. It has changed its language. Political movements, national identities, secular ideologies, professional communities, and online groups can function as complete worlds. Each develops its own authorities, sacred values, forbidden questions, and stories about who threatens the community. A person may reject traditional religion while remaining enclosed within another system that divides humanity into the enlightened and the ignorant, the innocent and the corrupt, or the people and their enemies.

The first challenge of reflection is therefore difficult precisely because it appears elementary. We must learn to see our world as a world, rather than as the unquestionable shape of reality.

## The Other Side of the Board

Consider a small child looking at a board that is red on one side and blue on the other. The child sees the red surface. An adult sitting across from the child sees blue. When asked what the adult sees, a very young child may answer “red,” because the child has not yet learned to separate personal perception from another person’s field of vision.

With greater maturity, the child understands that the same object can present different appearances from different positions. The adult is not confused or dishonest. The adult is looking from the other side.

This elementary capacity contains the beginning of a profound human achievement. To enter another person’s perspective, I must loosen the assumption that my immediate experience is shared by everyone. I remain aware of what I see, but I can also imagine a position from which the object appears differently. My perspective does not vanish. It ceases to occupy the entire field.

Something similar occurs when we encounter another culture with sustained attention. Practices that first appear irrational may have developed in response to a climate, a history, a social structure, or a collective wound unlike our own. Religious beliefs that seem strange from the outside may carry memories and meanings that are invisible to the visitor. Customs inherited without question in one society may appear equally strange when viewed from another.

Such recognition can produce a healthy humility. I may love Japanese food while understanding why someone raised elsewhere prefers Chinese, Filipino, Indian, or Italian food. My attachment does not require a universal argument. Difference can remain difference without becoming a contest for supremacy.

The analogy reaches its limit when we turn to moral and political conflict. Immigration is not only a matter of cultural preference. It concerns borders, labor, security, family separation, economic opportunity, and the dignity of people whose lives may be shaped by decisions made far from them. Climate change cannot be reduced to competing lifestyles because physical conditions and measurable consequences constrain what can reasonably be claimed. Questions involving LGBT people concern not only traditional beliefs but the safety, freedom, and social recognition of actual human beings. Human rights become meaningful because certain actions affect bodies, relationships, opportunities, and lives.

Perspective-taking does not require us to treat every claim as equally sound. It asks us to understand the position from which a claim becomes persuasive before we judge it. An explanation is not an endorsement. Empathy does not abolish moral discernment. It may instead make judgment more accurate by preventing us from condemning an imaginary version of the other person.

The meta-perspective begins here. A Christian can recognize Christianity as a historical tradition interpreted through many cultures and denominations. A Buddhist can examine the institutions, practices, and inherited concepts through which Buddhism has been transmitted. A secular humanist can see that humanism also carries a history and a set of assumptions about reason, freedom, and the person.

This recognition need not destroy commitment. It changes the way commitment is held. Faith can remain deep while admitting that its human expressions are limited. Cultural belonging can remain strong without becoming a measure of another person’s worth. Conviction no longer requires the fantasy that we stand outside history.

## The Search for Common Ground

The modern world attempted to create a shared ground across religious and cultural divisions. The Enlightenment placed growing confidence in reason, observation, mathematics, and publicly accessible evidence. Instead of resolving every dispute through scripture, inherited authority, or revelation, people could ask what could be demonstrated to others regardless of faith.

Science became one of the most powerful forms of this aspiration. Its strength did not come from scientists becoming free of culture, ambition, error, or prejudice. Scientists remained human. The strength came from methods that exposed individual judgment to correction. Claims could be measured, tested, criticized, repeated, revised, or abandoned. Knowledge became accountable to evidence that others could inspect.

Objectivity, understood in this way, is not a location occupied by a perfectly detached mind. It is an achievement produced through disciplined cooperation. No individual sees the whole object, but different observers can compare results and identify errors that would remain invisible within a private worldview. The process does not remove perspective. It organizes encounters among perspectives so that reality can challenge them.

Mathematics offers another form of common ground. People may disagree about theology or political authority while reaching the same result through a valid calculation. Shared methods provide a language through which disagreement can be narrowed. They cannot answer every human question, but they make certain forms of agreement possible.

Modern ideas of human rights grew from a related hope. Human beings would possess moral standing that did not depend entirely on citizenship, religion, ethnicity, sex, wealth, or social rank. A person could appeal to a principle beyond the traditions of a particular community when that community inflicted cruelty or denied basic freedom.

Human rights have a history. Their modern language emerged through specific philosophical, political, and legal developments, and their application has often been selective. Nations that proclaimed universal dignity also practiced colonial domination, racial exclusion, and economic exploitation. The contradiction deserves attention, but it does not erase the moral aspiration. A principle can exceed the failures of the people who first expressed it.

The search for universality therefore accomplished something indispensable. It created standards by which inherited authority could be questioned. Tradition alone could no longer justify torture, enslavement, exclusion, or domination. Institutions could be asked to provide reasons that reached beyond the interests of those already holding power.

Still, the universal standpoint carried its own temptation. A society could treat its local version of reason as reason itself. A dominant culture could present its assumptions as neutral while describing other cultures as trapped in superstition or backwardness. The effort to escape enclosure could create another enclosure, this time protected by the language of progress and objectivity.

Modernity taught us to question inherited worlds. It did not free us from the human desire to place ourselves at the center of history.

## When Every View Becomes Situated

Postmodern criticism arose partly from the recognition that claims of neutrality often conceal power. Knowledge is produced within institutions. Research depends on funding, professional incentives, accepted methods, available technologies, and judgments about which questions deserve attention. Language shapes the categories through which reality becomes intelligible. Even careful observation begins from assumptions about what counts as relevant.

This criticism corrected an inflated picture of objectivity. The observer does not float above history. Every human being sees from somewhere, through a body, a language, a culture, and a set of inherited concepts. The categories used to classify others may reveal as much about the classifier as about the people being classified.

The same scrutiny applies to universal morality. Appeals to civilization, development, security, or human rights can serve genuine moral purposes, but they can also disguise political interests. A powerful country may describe its own priorities as universal while treating the priorities of weaker societies as local obstacles. A majority may invoke neutrality while preserving arrangements that have always favored it.

Recognizing these conditions deepens intellectual responsibility. We learn to ask who created a category, whose experience it reflects, who benefits from its use, and whose voice was absent when it became authoritative. Such questions do not refute a claim by themselves. They reveal dimensions that a claim may have concealed.

Relativism becomes dangerous when this insight is extended into the belief that no claim can be better than another. If every statement is reduced to an expression of power or identity, evidence loses its authority. A documented fact becomes one narrative among many. A moral protest becomes only the preference of a particular group. The critique of domination can then weaken our ability to identify domination at all.

Reality does not become optional because our access to it is limited. A changing climate produces effects that do not depend on political preference. A person subjected to violence experiences consequences that cannot be dissolved by describing morality as culturally constructed. Historical records may remain incomplete, but incompleteness does not make every account equally credible.

We need distinctions among different kinds of disagreement. Some questions depend heavily on empirical evidence. Others concern competing moral goods, such as liberty, equality, security, loyalty, and care. Still others involve the distribution of power or the interpretation of inherited symbols. Confusion grows when one kind of claim is treated as another. Evidence cannot determine every moral priority, while cultural sensitivity cannot decide whether a physical measurement is accurate.

A mature pluralism holds two recognitions together. Human knowledge is situated and fallible, yet the world can resist our interpretations. Moral languages arise within history, yet suffering and dignity make claims upon us across cultural boundaries. We do not have to choose between possessing absolute certainty and abandoning the search for truth.

The meta-perspective allows us to hold these tensions, but it introduces another danger. Once we learn to see the limits of every worldview, we may become fascinated with our own ability to see them.

## The Ego Above the Conflict

A person who steps outside an inherited worldview may experience a powerful sense of release. Doctrines that once appeared unquestionable become historical constructions. Conflicts that once demanded loyalty can be examined from a distance. Other traditions become intelligible, and former enemies become fellow human beings shaped by different circumstances.

The liberation is real. So is the temptation that follows it.

The person who recognizes many perspectives may begin to feel superior to those who remain committed to one. Believers appear naive, conservatives appear trapped by tradition, progressives appear captured by ideology, and ordinary citizens appear unable to understand the larger system. The observer believes that everyone else occupies a limited viewpoint while they alone perceive the structure containing them all.

The meta-perspective has then become another enclosure. Its boundaries are harder to detect because openness, tolerance, and sophistication form part of its self-image. Earlier worldviews announced their absolutes directly. The new worldview may claim to have no absolutes while treating its own pluralism as unquestionable.

Tolerance can become intolerant toward people judged insufficiently tolerant. Critics of tribalism can form a tribe united by contempt for those they regard as tribal. Advocates of inclusion can exclude anyone whose language does not match the accepted vocabulary of inclusion. Defenders of reason can use ridicule in place of reasoning when speaking about religious people. No intellectual position cancels the human appetite for status and belonging.

A further movement of reflection is needed. I must recognize not only that my original worldview was a perspective, but also that my apparent transcendence of it remains a perspective. My capacity to interpret other people does not place me outside interpretation. While I examine their limitations, they may see limitations in me that my position prevents me from noticing.

This is the meta-meta-perspective. The name can sound like another level in a hierarchy, as though consciousness were climbing to a higher floor. Its deeper movement goes in the opposite direction. It releases the desire to stand on the highest floor.

Such awareness does not create a final position from which all views become visible. Human finitude remains. I still speak a language, carry memories, inhabit a body, and belong to a time and place. Reflection enlarges my field of vision, but it does not turn me into a view from nowhere.

Nor does the meta-meta-perspective require endless intellectual recursion. We could continue indefinitely by examining the perspective that examines the perspective. Contemplative awareness has a more practical purpose. It catches the moment when reflection becomes possession, when humility becomes identity, and when openness becomes a reason to feel superior.

I can remain convinced while asking how my conviction is shaping what I notice. I can criticize another position while considering what its adherents see that I do not. I can defend a moral boundary without imagining that crossing it makes another person less human. These disciplines do not weaken judgment. They protect judgment from becoming an instrument of vanity.

## Surrendering the Achievement of Surrender

Contemplative traditions have long confronted the difficulty of a self attempting to transcend itself. Zen practice, Buddhist meditation, Christian mysticism, Sufi devotion, and other paths use different languages and arise from different understandings of reality. They should not be collapsed into one universal system. Yet many share a concern with attachment, self-importance, and the urge to possess spiritual truth.

The contemplative person may begin by surrendering ordinary ambitions. Recognition, wealth, control, and social approval lose some of their power. Attention becomes less occupied with protecting an image of the self. Prayer or meditation opens a space in which experience no longer revolves entirely around personal desire.

Then a subtler attachment emerges. The self takes possession of its surrender.

A person may begin to think, “I have become detached,” “I have overcome ego,” or “I have reached a higher state of consciousness.” The spiritual experience becomes evidence of identity. Humility turns into an accomplishment, and selflessness becomes a distinction separating the awakened from the unawakened.

There is no contradiction in becoming aware that one has acted without self-interest. Human beings can reflect on their motives and recognize changes in their conduct. The trap appears when the recognition becomes ownership. Instead of receiving the experience and allowing it to pass, the person uses it to construct a stable image of being selfless.

Even surrender may therefore need to be surrendered. The person releases not only control but the satisfaction of being someone who has released control. This movement cannot be displayed without risk, because public recognition can return the experience to the economy of status. The self wears contemplative language while continuing its search for importance.

Institutions face a related difficulty. A transformative experience is remembered and described. Practices are developed to help others encounter it. Teachers preserve those practices, communities gather around them, and lineages protect them from distortion. Each step may be necessary. Without institutions, wisdom can disappear.

Over time, however, the vessel may begin to identify itself with what it carries. A school claims that its method is the highest method. A denomination treats its interpretation as the only faithful interpretation. A contemplative community compares its depth with the supposed superficiality of ordinary religion. A path designed to loosen attachment becomes an object of attachment.

Zen traditions have divided into schools. Christian communities formed around humility have fought over doctrine and authority. Movements created to recover spiritual freedom have developed their own hierarchies, orthodoxies, and exclusions. These outcomes do not prove that the original practices were false. They show how persistently human beings convert insight into possession.

Contemplation cannot be guaranteed by membership in a contemplative tradition. It is also not secured by rejecting institutions and declaring oneself spiritually independent. The independent seeker can become as attached to autonomy as the institutional believer is attached to doctrine. Every form carries possibilities for discipline and possibilities for self-deception.

A more credible understanding treats contemplation as repeated relinquishment rather than permanent attainment. The self returns. It interprets what happened, takes credit for progress, and builds another identity. Awareness notices the construction and releases it again. There is no certificate confirming that the work has been completed.

This may sound discouraging, but it also removes an unnecessary burden. We do not have to become beings who never experience ego, attachment, certainty, or tribal loyalty. We need the capacity to recognize these movements and loosen their hold. Freedom appears through the practice of returning, not through possession of a flawless state.

## Hope Among Unfinished Perspectives

Contemporary societies bring incompatible worlds into constant contact. Religious doctrines, national memories, scientific claims, personal identities, economic interests, and moral demands meet in the same public space. Digital communication compresses distance and accelerates reaction. People who once might never have encountered one another now argue about the meaning of family, freedom, justice, citizenship, history, and the human person.

The resulting conflict can feel endless. One side defends tradition against cultural dissolution. Another defends vulnerable people against inherited exclusion. One group treats national borders as the foundation of political responsibility. Another sees the desperate movement of migrants as a demand that cannot be answered by sovereignty alone. Each position contains fears, loyalties, and moral insights, but public debate often rewards the sharpest accusation rather than the fullest understanding.

A reflective perspective will not erase these conflicts. Climate policy still requires decisions. Immigration law still creates boundaries and consequences. Religious freedom may collide with claims for equal treatment. Societies must decide how to protect both expression and human dignity. Refusing to judge is itself a decision that often favors existing power.

The change occurs in the manner of judgment. We can oppose a belief without reducing the believer to that belief. We can defend the rights of LGBT people without treating every religious person as motivated by hatred. We can take concerns about social cohesion seriously without describing immigrants as contaminants. We can acknowledge the historical limits of human-rights language while continuing to use it against cruelty and exclusion.

The meta-meta-perspective keeps every participant within the field of examination. Traditionalists must examine whether appeals to heritage conceal fear or inherited domination. Reformers must ask whether moral confidence has become contempt for those whose lives were formed by another language. Scientific authorities must remain accountable to evidence and criticism. Critics of science must distinguish legitimate scrutiny from the convenience of rejecting unwelcome facts.

No group receives an exemption from self-examination. Yet no group is denied the possibility of contributing insight. A person can be wrong about one matter without becoming worthless. A tradition can carry harmful assumptions while preserving forms of wisdom that modern societies need. A movement can pursue justice while remaining vulnerable to dogmatism. Moral seriousness grows when these mixed realities are faced without demanding purity.

Contemplation becomes a public discipline through the interval it creates between conviction and retaliation. In that interval, I can ask whether I have understood the other person’s claim, whether fear has simplified my picture of them, whether loyalty to my group is directing my judgment, and whether the language of principle is concealing a desire to win. The questions do not settle every dispute. They change the person entering it.

Hope does not depend on a future in which humanity finally agrees about religion, culture, morality, or political order. History offers little reason to expect the disappearance of conflict. Difference belongs to the conditions of human life, and new circumstances will continue to produce new disagreements.

A stronger hope rests on our ability to change the character of conflict. Disagreement need not become dehumanization. Conviction need not become fanaticism. Membership need not require blindness, and openness need not become superiority. Human beings can learn to see another side while remaining responsible for the side from which they act.

The child begins by seeing red and assuming that everyone sees the same color. Maturity recognizes that another person sees blue. Deeper reflection resists the temptation to claim a complete view from above the board. Each of us remains somewhere, seeing something real but incomplete, dependent on others to disclose what our position hides.

We may never possess the final perspective. That limitation does not condemn us to nihilism. It invites a different relationship with truth, one grounded in commitment, correction, and humility. We can seek what is true without turning truth into property. We can inhabit a tradition without making it the boundary of humanity. We can surrender without constructing an identity around surrender.

Freedom begins again whenever we release the need to stand above everyone else. Hope survives in the willingness to repeat that release, especially when the world gives us countless reasons to return to our tribes.
